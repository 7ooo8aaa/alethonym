eSIM vs Pocket WiFi vs SIM Card: Which Is Best for Japan?

Updated: 2026-09-17 · Alethonym

Before you even get to "which eSIM plan," there's an earlier question: should it be an eSIM at all? Physical SIM cards and pocket WiFi routers are both still normal ways to get online in Japan, and each fits a different kind of trip. This guide starts from that decision, then narrows down to what to actually check once you've picked a lane.

※ Pricing, carrier coverage, and specific plan details change often and vary by provider. This article focuses on how to decide between the three options — always confirm current pricing and terms on the provider's own site before you buy.

What is an eSIM, briefly

An eSIM (embedded SIM) is a mobile plan written to a chip already built into your phone, instead of a physical card you insert. You typically install it by scanning a QR code or through an app, which requires an internet connection — so it's usually set up before you travel, over WiFi at home, then activated once you land.

A simple way to decide

  • Traveling solo or as a couple, each with your own unlocked phone: an eSIM is usually the least hassle — no counter visit, no device to return.
  • Traveling as a group or family sharing one connection: a pocket WiFi router can be cheaper per person and avoids buying a separate plan for everyone.
  • Your phone doesn't support eSIM, or isn't unlocked: a physical SIM or pocket WiFi are your realistic options — check unlock status before you fly, since some carrier-locked phones can't use either a foreign SIM or an eSIM from a different provider.
  • You want a backup you can hand to someone else, or use with a tablet/laptop too: pocket WiFi covers multiple devices at once; an eSIM or physical SIM only serves the phone it's installed in (unless you use tethering).

How much data do you actually need?

This depends heavily on how you use your phone, not just trip length. As a rough starting point for maps, messaging apps, and light social media use, many travelers find 1–2GB per day is enough; heavier use — video calls, streaming, or uploading a lot of photos/video — pushes that higher. If you're unsure, an unlimited or high-cap plan, or a provider that lets you top up mid-trip, removes the guesswork.

Coverage: cities vs. rural areas

Mobile coverage in central Tokyo, Osaka, and other major cities is generally reliable across providers. If your trip includes rural areas, small islands, or mountain regions (parts of Hokkaido, national parks, some stretches of the Japan Alps), coverage can be patchier — this is worth checking against the specific carrier network a provider uses before you rely on it somewhere remote, since providers can differ here.

Trip length: does it change the answer?

  • A few days, city-focused: eSIM or pocket WiFi both work well; airport SIM pickup can also make sense if you're arriving during counter hours.
  • One to two weeks, moving between cities: eSIM avoids having to return a router at the right location before a different-city departure; check the data cap is enough for the whole trip or that top-ups are available.
  • Longer stays or repeat trips: compare whether a longer-validity plan, or a local physical SIM bought after arrival, works out simpler than a short-trip eSIM plan.

Setting up before you arrive

  1. Confirm your phone supports eSIM (if going that route) and is carrier-unlocked.
  2. Buy the plan and install the eSIM profile at home, over WiFi — this step needs an internet connection, so don't leave it for the airport.
  3. Don't activate it yet unless the provider's instructions say otherwise — many plans start their validity countdown from activation, not installation.
  4. Note the manual steps you'll need on arrival: switching your data line to the new eSIM, and turning on data roaming for it specifically (this trips up a lot of first-time users).

Using mobile data after you land

On arrival, switch on airplane mode briefly if your phone is slow to reconnect, then confirm your phone's mobile data line is set to the new eSIM (not your home SIM) and that data roaming is enabled for it. Keep your home SIM active for calls/SMS if you want to keep receiving them, with its own data turned off to avoid roaming charges.

At the airport

If you've already installed an eSIM at home, there's nothing left to do at the airport beyond activating it once you're through immigration — no counter, no queue. If you're going the physical SIM or pocket WiFi route instead, both are commonly available at major airports (Narita, Haneda, Kansai and others), but counter hours and stock can vary, so it's worth having a backup plan if you're landing very early, very late, or need something specific to be in stock.

Common mistakes

  • Installing the eSIM after landing: you may not have WiFi to do it, and airport WiFi isn't always reliable enough for the setup step.
  • Forgetting to switch data roaming on for the new eSIM specifically, then assuming the eSIM "doesn't work."
  • Leaving the home SIM's data on alongside the eSIM, resulting in unexpected roaming charges.
  • Assuming a locked phone can use any option: some carrier-locked phones can't accept a foreign SIM or a different provider's eSIM — check before you fly, not after you land.
  • Not checking refund/cancellation terms before buying — many providers don't refund a plan once it's been activated or used.

Frequently asked questions

Do I really need an eSIM for Japan?

Not necessarily. If you're only in cities, rarely leaving your group, and comfortable relying on free WiFi at hotels, cafes, and stations, you can get by with less. Most travelers who want maps, translation, and ride-hailing apps to work reliably choose some form of mobile data, and an eSIM is simply one way to get it.

Will my phone number still work if I use a data-only eSIM?

Yes, on a dual-SIM phone. Your home SIM stays in the phone for calls and texts (usually with data roaming turned off to avoid charges), while the eSIM handles data. You can typically still receive SMS-based verification codes tied to your home number.

Can I set up an eSIM before I leave home?

Most eSIM providers let you install the profile before departure, over WiFi, so it's ready to activate on arrival. Whether the plan's validity period starts at installation or at first use in Japan depends on the provider — check this before you buy.

What if my phone doesn't support eSIM?

Older or budget phones, and some phones sold locked to a carrier, may not support eSIM. In that case, a physical SIM card or a pocket WiFi router are the practical alternatives.

Summary

  • Start from your trip shape (solo vs. group, city-only vs. rural, short vs. long) before comparing specific providers.
  • eSIM usually wins on convenience for solo/couple travelers with compatible, unlocked phones.
  • Pocket WiFi usually wins for groups sharing one connection or multiple devices.
  • Physical SIM remains a fine fallback, especially if your phone can't use eSIM.
  • Whichever you choose, install/prepare it before you fly, and confirm current pricing and coverage directly with the provider.
